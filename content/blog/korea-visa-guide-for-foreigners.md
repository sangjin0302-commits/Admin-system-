Applying for a visa in Korea can feel complex — the categories are many, the required documents change often, and immigration offices apply the rules strictly. This guide gives foreigners a clear, plain-English overview of how the Korean visa system works and where professional help makes the biggest difference.

> This article is general guidance, not legal advice. Individual eligibility is reviewed case by case after consultation.

## Who this guide is for

- Foreign professionals, founders, and students planning to live or work in Korea
- Current residents changing or extending their status
- Employers and sponsors preparing documents for a foreign hire

## Main Korean visa categories

Korea groups visas by purpose. The most common categories for foreigners include:

| Category | Purpose | Typical holders |
|----------|---------|-----------------|
| **D-2 / D-4** | Study, language training | Students, exchange participants |
| **D-8** | Business / startup investment | Founders, corporate investors |
| **D-10** | Job seeking, startup preparation | Graduates, prospective founders |
| **E-7** | Designated professional work | Skilled specialists sponsored by an employer |
| **F-2 / F-5** | Long-term residence / permanent residence | Point-based residents, long-term settlers |
| **F-6** | Marriage to a Korean national | Spouses |

Each category has its own eligibility test, and many applicants qualify for more than one — choosing the right path is often the single most important decision.

## What immigration actually checks

Regardless of category, Korean immigration reviews four things:

1. **Eligibility** — do you meet the category's specific requirements (income, education, sponsorship, points)?
2. **Documentation** — are your documents complete, recent, and correctly certified/translated?
3. **Genuineness** — is the stated purpose consistent with your history and supporting evidence?
4. **Compliance** — is your current status in good standing (no overstays or violations)?

Most refusals come from documentation gaps, not from applicants being unqualified.

## Documents you will usually need

- Valid passport and current Alien Registration Card (if already in Korea)
- Category-specific proof (employment contract, business registration, enrollment, income records)
- Certified translations of foreign-issued documents
- Application forms and fee payment

Requirements differ by category and change over time, so always confirm the current checklist before filing.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- Applying under the wrong category because it "looks easier"
- Submitting documents older than the accepted issue window (often 30 days)
- Missing certified translations or notarization
- Letting your current status lapse before filing a change or extension
